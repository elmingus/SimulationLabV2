Bedside Assessment and Instrumentation

Choosing the Appropriate Swallowing Assessment

Begin with the clinical question.

Do not choose an assessment simply because it is familiar or readily available.

SLP Task

For each scenario, determine whether a clinical swallow evaluation, FEES, MBSS, or a combination is most appropriate. Explain what information the selected assessment can and cannot provide.

Screening, Clinical Evaluation, and Diagnosis

These terms are related but are not interchangeable.

Swallow Screening

A screening identifies patients who may be at risk for dysphagia and require further assessment.

It is not a complete diagnostic evaluation.

Clinical Swallow Evaluation

A clinical swallow evaluation may include:

  • Review of medical history
  • Respiratory status
  • Mental status
  • Communication abilities
  • Oral mechanism examination
  • Secretion management
  • Voice and cough
  • Oral trials when appropriate
  • Clinical recommendations
  • Determination of whether instrumentation is indicated

Instrumental Assessment

Instrumental assessment allows direct observation of swallowing anatomy, bolus flow, airway invasion, residue, and the effects of selected strategies.

The two primary instrumental assessments are:

  • Fiberoptic Endoscopic Evaluation of Swallowing
  • Modified Barium Swallow Study

Clinical Swallow Evaluation

Strengths

  • Available at bedside
  • Provides essential medical and functional context
  • Evaluates readiness and ability to participate
  • Allows assessment of oral motor function
  • Supports immediate clinical recommendations
  • Helps determine whether instrumentation is necessary

Limitations

A bedside evaluation cannot directly visualize:

  • Aspiration
  • Silent aspiration
  • Pharyngeal residue
  • Laryngeal penetration
  • Timing of airway invasion
  • Pharyngeal biomechanics

A patient may demonstrate aspiration without coughing or other obvious bedside signs.

FEES

FEES May Be Especially Helpful When You Need To Evaluate

  • Secretion management
  • Laryngeal anatomy
  • Vocal fold movement
  • Airway protection
  • Penetration or aspiration
  • Pharyngeal residue
  • Fatigue across repeated trials
  • Swallowing at bedside
  • A patient who cannot easily leave the ICU
  • Response to strategies without radiation exposure
  • Changes over repeated examinations

Limitations

  • The oral phase is not directly visualized.
  • The moment of the swallow is obscured by white-out.
  • The cervical esophagus is not evaluated.
  • Nasal passage of the scope may be uncomfortable.
  • Specialized equipment and trained clinicians are required.

MBSS

MBSS May Be Especially Helpful When You Need To Evaluate

  • Oral bolus preparation and transport
  • Timing and coordination across swallowing phases
  • Hyolaryngeal movement
  • Pharyngeal clearance
  • Penetration or aspiration
  • Timing of airway invasion
  • Upper esophageal opening
  • Effects of bolus volume, consistency, posture, or strategy
  • Swallowing biomechanics across lateral and anterior-posterior views

Limitations

  • Requires radiation exposure.
  • Usually requires transport to radiology.
  • Represents a limited sample of swallowing performance.
  • Secretions are not directly visualized.
  • Vocal fold movement is not directly assessed.

What Did the Screening Review Find?

The Bours systematic review examined bedside screening methods for adults with neurological disorders.

Major findings included:

  • Water swallow tests demonstrated variable sensitivity and specificity.
  • Tests using several endpoints performed better than isolated clinical signs.
  • Single findings such as an abnormal gag were generally not sufficiently accurate when used alone.
  • The review identified a water swallow test combined with pulse oximetry and observation of coughing, choking, or voice change as the most promising screening approach among the studies reviewed.
  • The authors emphasized the need for standardized screening and instrumental protocols.

This review was published in 2009 and focused primarily on screening patients with neurological disorders. It should not be interpreted as establishing pulse oximetry as a stand-alone measure of aspiration.

Choose the Assessment

Scenario 1

A patient in the ICU has copious secretions, a weak voice, and cannot safely leave the unit.

Reveal Discussion

FEES may be particularly useful.

It can be completed at bedside and allows direct assessment of secretions, laryngeal anatomy, airway protection, and pharyngeal residue.

A clinical swallow evaluation remains necessary for medical context and readiness.

Scenario 2

A patient following partial glossectomy demonstrates prolonged oral preparation and suspected reduced bolus propulsion.

Reveal Discussion

MBSS may be particularly useful when the primary question involves oral bolus preparation, oral transport, and bolus flow across swallowing phases.

FEES may also provide valuable information if secretion management, laryngeal function, or bedside assessment is the primary concern.

The correct selection depends on the clinical question.

Scenario 3

A patient with acute stroke has not yet received any swallowing assessment and is alert, medically stable, and able to participate.

Reveal Discussion

Begin with a clinical swallow evaluation unless the medical presentation or institutional pathway indicates immediate instrumentation.

Use the clinical assessment to identify risks, immediate recommendations, and unanswered questions.

Scenario 4

A patient demonstrates no coughing with liquids but has recurrent pneumonia and an unexplained oxygen requirement.

Reveal Discussion

The absence of coughing does not rule out aspiration.

Instrumentation should be considered because the bedside examination cannot reliably exclude silent aspiration.

FEES or MBSS may be appropriate depending on the specific clinical question, transport feasibility, and patient status.

Scenario 5

A patient can tolerate only several minutes of transportation and positioning but requires repeated assessment of secretion management and airway protection.

Reveal Discussion

FEES may be preferred because it can be performed at bedside, directly visualizes secretions, and can be repeated without radiation exposure.

Clinical Pearl

Instrumentation should answer a question that cannot be answered through bedside assessment alone.
