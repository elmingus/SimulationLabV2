Oral Mechanism Case Practice

Case Practice

Complete a focused oral mechanism examination for each patient. The emphasis is on examination technique, expected findings, and clinical reasoning.

SLP Task

For each case:

  1. Identify the highest-priority components of the oral mechanism examination.
  2. Identify relevant cranial nerves.
  3. Describe findings you may reasonably anticipate.
  4. Avoid assuming that an expected deficit is present before completing the examination.

Case A: John

Patient Snapshot

John is a 65-year-old man with a medical history significant for alcohol use disorder and a recent diagnosis of base-of-tongue squamous cell carcinoma.

A tracheostomy tube was placed immediately before surgery due to concern for airway protection and anticipated postoperative edema.

John is now postoperative day 2 following partial glossectomy. He is tolerating room air. The medical team consulted SLP for evaluation and treatment.

Consider

  • What structures were directly affected by surgery?
  • Which oral mechanism findings are highest priority?
  • How might postoperative edema affect the examination?
  • What information would you gather about the tracheostomy?
  • What communication and swallowing concerns may be present?
  • Would a bedside examination be sufficient to answer all clinical questions?
Clinical Reasoning Prompts

Priority areas may include:

  • Tongue appearance, range of motion, strength, and symmetry
  • Surgical site and postoperative changes
  • Oral containment and secretion management
  • Voice and cough
  • Tracheostomy type, cuff status, and presence of a speaking valve
  • Respiratory stability
  • Ability to follow directions
  • Need for instrumental swallowing assessment

Do not infer swallowing safety from room-air tolerance alone.

Case B: Lucy

Patient Snapshot

Lucy is an 85-year-old woman who experienced a right-hemisphere stroke.

She presents with left-sided hemiparesis and reduced sensation on the left side. She is currently NPO while awaiting SLP evaluation.

Nursing reports no observed swallowing difficulty but notes that Lucy typically takes multiple pills simultaneously.

Consider

  • Which side of the face and oral cavity may be affected?
  • How will you assess facial and lingual sensation?
  • How might reduced awareness or attention affect the examination?
  • Does the absence of nursing concern rule out dysphagia?
  • What additional cognitive or perceptual findings may influence oral intake?
  • Is taking multiple pills at once appropriate during the initial evaluation?
Clinical Reasoning Prompts

Priority areas may include:

  • Facial symmetry and labial closure
  • Oral sensation
  • Tongue range of motion and strength
  • Oral awareness and residue detection
  • Attention to the left side
  • Alertness and ability to follow safety directions
  • Voice, cough, and secretion management
  • Medication administration practices

A right-hemisphere stroke may also affect attention, awareness, judgment, and self-monitoring. Findings must be confirmed through assessment rather than assumed.

Case C: Ken

Patient Snapshot

Ken is a 43-year-old man who experienced a left-hemisphere stroke.

He has just been admitted to the ICU from the emergency department. He is a poor historian, and his past medical history is limited.

Aphasia is suspected. He is awaiting SLP evaluation.

Consider

  • How will suspected aphasia affect your examination?
  • How can you distinguish difficulty understanding directions from motor impairment?
  • What communication supports could improve participation?
  • Which commands could be demonstrated rather than explained verbally?
  • What collateral information is needed before oral trials?
  • What aspects of the examination should be deferred if the patient cannot participate reliably?
Clinical Reasoning Prompts

Consider using:

  • One-step directions
  • Visual demonstration
  • Gestures
  • Yes/no questions
  • Written keywords when appropriate
  • Additional processing time
  • Repetition or rephrasing
  • Information from nursing, family, and the medical record

Document when examination results are limited by impaired comprehension, reduced alertness, or inconsistent participation.

Case D: Paul

Patient Snapshot

Paul is a 36-year-old man admitted following surgery.

He initially presented to a walk-in clinic with an 8-mm lesion on the left interior lower lip. Biopsy revealed squamous cell carcinoma of the lower lip, resulting in lower labial resection.

Paul is doing well in recovery and is awaiting SLP evaluation.

Consider

  • What structural changes may affect oral function?
  • How will you assess labial closure?
  • What speech sounds may be affected?
  • How might the resection affect oral containment?
  • What compensatory strategies may be considered?
  • What additional information is needed regarding reconstruction, pain, edema, and surgical precautions?
Clinical Reasoning Prompts

Priority areas may include:

  • Lip symmetry and range of motion
  • Labial strength and seal
  • Anterior containment
  • Oral pressure
  • Speech intelligibility
  • Pain and edema
  • Sensation near the surgical site
  • Surgical precautions and wound integrity

Avoid manipulating the surgical site without confirming postoperative restrictions.

Documentation Practice

Choose one case and write a brief objective statement.

Use this structure:

Oral mechanism examination revealed ________. Findings may contribute to ________. Assessment was limited by ________.

Then write one recommendation:

Recommend ________ to further evaluate or address ________.
