Cranial Nerves and Oral Mechanism Examination

Cranial Nerve Review

A focused oral mechanism examination helps the SLP describe the integrity and function of structures involved in speech and swallowing.

SLP Task

Work with a partner. One person performs the examination while the other identifies the cranial nerve, expected response, and possible functional implications.

Before You Begin

A cranial nerve examination is only one component of the clinical assessment.

An isolated abnormal finding does not independently diagnose dysphagia or aspiration. Interpret findings alongside the patient’s medical history, respiratory status, cognition, communication abilities, secretion management, and swallowing presentation.

Cranial Nerves Relevant to Speech and Swallowing

Cranial Nerve Primary Functions Focused Assessment Possible Functional Relevance
CN V: Trigeminal Jaw movement; facial and oral sensation Open and close jaw; clench teeth; assess jaw strength and symmetry; assess facial sensation when indicated Mastication, oral preparation, awareness of material in the oral cavity
CN VII: Facial Facial movement; lip closure; taste to anterior tongue Smile; pucker; retract lips; puff cheeks; assess facial symmetry Labial seal, anterior containment, oral pressure
CN IX: Glossopharyngeal Pharyngeal sensation; taste to posterior tongue; contribution to pharyngeal movement Consider sensory response and patient report; gag testing is not routinely necessary Pharyngeal sensation and sensory input for swallowing
CN X: Vagus Palatal movement; phonation; laryngeal function; cough; pharyngeal movement Observe palate during sustained “ah”; assess voice quality; voluntary cough; secretion management Airway protection, vocal fold function, pharyngeal movement
CN XI: Spinal Accessory Head turn and shoulder elevation Shoulder shrug; turn head against resistance Positioning and ability to use some postural strategies
CN XII: Hypoglossal Tongue movement Protrude tongue; lateralize; elevate; depress; assess strength and symmetry Bolus formation, manipulation, propulsion, oral clearance

Quiz Yourself

Try to answer each item before revealing the response.

Question 1

A patient presents with reduced lip closure and anterior loss of liquids.

Reveal Answer

CN VII: Facial

CN VII contributes to facial movement and labial closure. Reduced function may affect anterior containment and generation of oral pressure.

Question 2

A patient demonstrates reduced tongue lateralization and difficulty clearing food from the lateral sulci.

Reveal Answer

CN XII: Hypoglossal

CN XII supports tongue movement for bolus manipulation, lateralization, propulsion, and oral clearance.

Question 3

A patient presents with a breathy voice and weak voluntary cough.

Reveal Answer

CN X: Vagus

These findings may raise concern for impaired laryngeal function or airway protection. They do not independently confirm aspiration.

Question 4

A patient demonstrates reduced jaw opening and weak mastication.

Reveal Answer

CN V: Trigeminal

CN V supports jaw movement and contributes to oral sensation.

Question 5

A patient is unable to maintain an upright head position or turn the head adequately.

Reveal Answer

CN XI: Spinal Accessory

CN XI supports shoulder elevation and head turning. Weakness may affect positioning and the feasibility of some compensatory postures.

Partner Practice

Complete a focused oral mechanism examination.

Face and Lips

  • Observe facial symmetry at rest.
  • Ask the patient to smile.
  • Ask the patient to pucker.
  • Ask the patient to puff the cheeks.
  • Assess lip strength and ability to maintain pressure.

Jaw

  • Observe jaw position at rest.
  • Ask the patient to open and close the jaw.
  • Assess lateral jaw movement.
  • Assess strength when clinically appropriate.

Tongue

  • Observe the tongue at rest.
  • Ask the patient to protrude the tongue.
  • Ask the patient to move the tongue left and right.
  • Ask the patient to elevate and depress the tongue.
  • Assess strength against resistance when appropriate.

Palate and Voice

  • Ask the patient to sustain “ah.”
  • Observe palatal movement and symmetry.
  • Listen to vocal quality.
  • Assess loudness and phonatory stability.
  • Ask for a voluntary cough or throat clear when appropriate.

Secretion Management

  • Observe the patient’s ability to manage saliva.
  • Note drooling, frequent suctioning, wet vocal quality, or visible secretions.
  • Consider alertness, respiratory status, and spontaneous swallowing frequency.

Clinical Pearl

Do not interpret the gag reflex as a stand-alone measure of swallowing safety. The Bours systematic review found that single clinical features, including an abnormal gag or volitional cough, generally demonstrated limited diagnostic usefulness when used alone.
