FEES Demonstration

Fiberoptic Endoscopic Evaluation of Swallowing

Today’s demonstration provides an introduction to the equipment, procedure, anatomy, and clinical reasoning involved in FEES.

SLP Task

Observe the procedure and identify:

  • Relevant anatomy
  • Secretion location and severity
  • Vocal fold movement
  • Airway protection
  • Penetration or aspiration
  • Pharyngeal residue
  • Response to strategies

Before the Examination

The clinician should review:

  • Reason for referral
  • Medical stability
  • Respiratory status
  • Oxygen requirements
  • Bleeding risk
  • Nasal or facial precautions
  • Recent surgery
  • Level of alertness
  • Ability to participate
  • Current diet and medication administration
  • Relevant imaging and previous swallowing assessments

Equipment

Common equipment may include:

  • Flexible endoscope
  • Light source and video system
  • Recording equipment
  • Personal protective equipment
  • Water-soluble lubricant
  • Food coloring when permitted by institutional protocol
  • Food and liquid consistencies
  • Suction equipment
  • Pulse oximetry when clinically indicated
  • Topical agents when permitted by institutional policy and clinician scope

Procedure Overview

1. Patient Preparation

  • Explain the procedure.
  • Confirm patient identity.
  • Review contraindications and precautions.
  • Position the patient.
  • Establish a method for communicating discomfort or requesting a pause.

2. Scope Passage

  • The scope is passed transnasally.
  • Nasal anatomy and patient tolerance are monitored.
  • The scope is advanced to obtain a view of the pharynx and larynx.

3. Anatomic and Functional Examination

The clinician may observe:

  • Base of tongue
  • Valleculae
  • Epiglottis
  • Arytenoids
  • False vocal folds
  • True vocal folds
  • Pyriform sinuses
  • Posterior pharyngeal wall
  • Secretion location
  • Spontaneous swallowing
  • Vocal fold movement
  • Cough or throat-clear response

4. Swallowing Trials

Trials are selected based on the clinical question and patient status.

The clinician may assess:

  • Premature spillage
  • Penetration
  • Aspiration before or after the swallow
  • Residue location and severity
  • Secretion management
  • Response to bolus modification
  • Response to posture or strategy
  • Fatigue across trials

What Is White-Out?

During the pharyngeal swallow, the endoscopic image is briefly obscured as the pharynx constricts around the scope.

This is referred to as white-out.

Because the exact moment of the swallow is not visible, airway invasion during the swallow is inferred from findings immediately before and after white-out.

What Can Be Seen?

Before the Swallow

  • Secretions
  • Premature spillage
  • Material entering the laryngeal vestibule before the swallow
  • Baseline airway status

After the Swallow

  • Pharyngeal residue
  • Laryngeal penetration
  • Aspiration below the vocal folds
  • Patient response to airway invasion
  • Effectiveness of clearing swallows or strategies

Observation Guide

Use the following prompts during the demonstration.

Anatomy

  • Are the structures symmetrical?
  • Is there edema?
  • Is there evidence of trauma or lesion?
  • Do the vocal folds abduct and adduct?

Secretions

  • Are secretions present?
  • Where are they located?
  • Does the patient respond?
  • Can the patient clear them?

Airway Protection

  • Is penetration present?
  • Is aspiration present?
  • Does the patient cough?
  • Is the response effective?
  • Is airway invasion cleared?

Efficiency

  • Is residue present?
  • Where is residue located?
  • Does it increase across trials?
  • Does an additional swallow reduce residue?

Strategy Testing

  • Does a postural adjustment improve performance?
  • Does changing volume or consistency improve performance?
  • Can the patient implement the strategy independently?

Stop or Pause Considerations

The examination may need to be paused or discontinued if the patient demonstrates:

  • Significant distress
  • Epistaxis
  • Respiratory instability
  • Reduced responsiveness
  • Inability to tolerate the scope
  • Significant agitation
  • A medical change requiring immediate attention

Follow institutional procedures and the supervising clinician’s direction.

Clinical Pearl

FEES is not simply a test for aspiration. It provides information about anatomy, secretions, sensation, airway protection, residue, fatigue, and response to intervention.

Reflection

After the demonstration, discuss:

  1. What information did FEES provide that a bedside evaluation could not?
  2. What information would MBSS provide that FEES did not?
  3. What findings changed the clinical recommendations?
  4. What aspects of the procedure require additional supervised training?
