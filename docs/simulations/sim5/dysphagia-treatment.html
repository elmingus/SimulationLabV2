Evidence-Based Dysphagia Treatment

Dysphagia Treatment in Acute and Critical Care

Treatment should be selected based on the patient’s swallowing physiology, medical status, goals, cognition, endurance, and ability to participate.

SLP Task

For each intervention, identify whether it is primarily compensatory, rehabilitative, or adjunctive. Be prepared to explain what impairment or clinical goal the intervention is intended to address.

What Did the Systematic Review Examine?

Duncan and colleagues reviewed randomized and quasi-randomized trials examining dysphagia interventions for adults in acute and critical care.

The review included:

  • 22 studies
  • Approximately 1,700 participants
  • 19 acute stroke studies
  • 2 intensive care studies involving patients with stroke and tracheostomy
  • 1 general intensive care study
  • 9 intervention categories

Most of the available evidence came from patients with acute stroke. Evidence specific to general critical care populations was limited.

Major Findings

Pneumonia

Swallowing treatment was associated with a reduced risk of pneumonia across the included studies.

The certainty of this evidence was rated as low, meaning the true effect may differ from the estimated effect.

Aspiration

The review did not find clear evidence that swallowing treatment reduced aspiration after treatment.

Return to Oral Intake

The review did not find clear evidence that treatment shortened the time required to return to oral intake.

Hospital Length of Stay

The review did not find clear evidence of a reduction in hospital length of stay.

Pharyngeal Residue

A small number of studies found improvement in pharyngeal residue severity, but the certainty of evidence was very low.

Overall Conclusion

The authors concluded that evidence supporting specific dysphagia interventions in acute and critical care remains limited.

Treatment Categories

Compensatory Strategies

Compensatory strategies are intended to improve swallowing safety or efficiency during use.

They do not necessarily create lasting physiologic change.

Examples may include:

  • Altering bolus volume
  • Altering rate of intake
  • Alternating solids and liquids
  • Upright positioning
  • Postural adjustments
  • Diet or liquid modification
  • Environmental modifications
  • Feeding assistance or supervision

A compensatory strategy should be selected because it addresses an identified clinical problem—not simply because it is commonly used.

Rehabilitative Interventions

Rehabilitative interventions are intended to produce longer-term changes in strength, coordination, range of motion, sensation, or swallowing physiology.

Interventions represented in the review included:

  • Tongue-palate resistance training
  • Respiratory muscle strength training
  • Chin tuck against resistance
  • Effortful swallowing exercises
  • Traditional swallowing exercises
  • Behavioral swallowing interventions

The review did not establish one exercise as universally effective for all acute care patients.

Sensory and Neurostimulation Interventions

The review also included:

  • Neuromuscular electrical stimulation
  • Pharyngeal electrical stimulation
  • Transcranial direct current stimulation
  • Transcranial magnetic stimulation
  • Acupuncture

Evidence varied considerably across interventions and outcomes. Several approaches remain specialized, emerging, or experimental within acute and critical care practice.

Clinical Reasoning Framework

Use the following sequence when developing a treatment plan.

1. Identify the Impairment

Examples:

  • Reduced lingual strength
  • Reduced bolus control
  • Reduced hyolaryngeal movement
  • Reduced pharyngeal clearance
  • Impaired airway closure
  • Reduced cough strength
  • Reduced endurance
  • Reduced sensation

2. Identify the Functional Consequence

Examples:

  • Anterior loss
  • Oral residue
  • Prolonged oral preparation
  • Pharyngeal residue
  • Penetration or aspiration
  • Inability to manage secretions
  • Inability to sustain a meal
  • Increased respiratory burden

3. Select an Intervention

Ask:

  • Does the intervention target the identified impairment?
  • Can the patient follow the directions?
  • Is the patient medically stable?
  • Can the patient complete the required intensity?
  • Is the treatment supported by instrumental findings?
  • Is the intervention appropriate for the current phase of recovery?
  • What outcome will be measured?

4. Reassess

Determine whether the intervention improves:

  • Swallow safety
  • Swallow efficiency
  • Oral intake
  • Secretion management
  • Patient comfort
  • Participation
  • Quality of life

Apply It

Scenario 1

A patient demonstrates reduced lingual strength and prolonged oral transit.

What intervention category might be appropriate?

Reveal Discussion

A rehabilitative intervention targeting lingual strength may be considered if the patient is medically stable and able to participate.

The specific exercise, intensity, and resistance should be individualized.

Scenario 2

A patient aspirates thin liquids during an instrumental assessment but demonstrates improved airway protection with a specific postural adjustment.

What intervention category is this?

Reveal Discussion

This is a compensatory strategy because it improves swallowing performance while the strategy is being used.

Scenario 3

A critically ill patient is significantly fatigued, inconsistently alert, and unable to follow multistep directions.

Would an intensive exercise program be appropriate today?

Reveal Discussion

Possibly not.

The treatment plan should reflect the patient’s medical stability, alertness, endurance, and capacity to participate. Initial management may instead prioritize positioning, oral care, secretion management, caregiver education, and reassessment.

Evidence Pearl

Absence of strong evidence does not mean that all treatment is ineffective. It means clinicians should avoid overstating certainty, select interventions based on a defensible physiologic rationale, and measure patient-centered outcomes.
