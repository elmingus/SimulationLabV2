Bedside Assessment and Instrumentation

Choosing the Appropriate Swallowing Assessment

Clinical swallowing assessment requires more than identifying whether a patient coughs during oral intake.

The SLP must consider the patient’s medical status, clinical presentation, and the specific questions that need to be answered.

Begin with the clinical question.

SLP Task

Review the strengths and limitations of clinical swallow evaluation, FEES, and MBSS.

For each scenario:

  1. Identify the primary clinical question.
  2. Determine which assessment approach is most appropriate.
  3. Explain what information the assessment can provide.
  4. Identify limitations or unanswered questions.
  5. Consider how the patient’s medical condition influences your decision.

Screening, Clinical Evaluation, and Instrumentation

These approaches serve different purposes and are not interchangeable.

Swallow Screening

Swallow screening identifies patients who may be at risk for dysphagia and require further evaluation.

Screening is not a comprehensive diagnostic assessment.

Clinical Swallow Evaluation (CSE)

A clinical swallow evaluation may include:

  • Review of medical and swallowing history
  • Respiratory status and medical stability
  • Alertness, cognition, and communication
  • Oral mechanism examination
  • Secretion management
  • Voice and cough
  • Oral trials when clinically appropriate
  • Clinical interpretation and recommendations
  • Determination of whether instrumental assessment is indicated

Instrumental Assessment

Instrumental swallowing assessment provides direct information about aspects of swallowing physiology that cannot be visualized during a bedside examination.

The two primary instrumental assessments are:

  • FEES: Fiberoptic Endoscopic Evaluation of Swallowing
  • MBSS: Modified Barium Swallow Study, also called VFSS

Clinical Swallow Evaluation

Strengths

  • Can be performed at bedside
  • Provides medical and functional context
  • Evaluates readiness and participation
  • Assesses oral structures and function
  • Identifies clinical signs that may raise concern for dysphagia
  • Supports immediate clinical recommendations
  • Helps determine whether instrumentation is indicated

Limitations

A clinical swallow evaluation cannot directly visualize:

  • Laryngeal penetration or aspiration
  • Pharyngeal residue
  • Timing of airway invasion
  • Pharyngeal swallowing biomechanics
  • Upper esophageal opening

The absence of coughing, wet vocal quality, or other overt clinical signs does not rule out aspiration.

Fiberoptic Endoscopic Evaluation of Swallowing (FEES)

FEES May Be Particularly Useful For

  • Secretion management
  • Laryngeal and pharyngeal anatomy
  • Vocal fold movement
  • Penetration and aspiration
  • Pharyngeal residue
  • Repeated swallowing trials
  • Fatigue across extended intake
  • Bedside assessment in the ICU
  • Patients who cannot easily be transported
  • Assessment of selected compensatory strategies
  • Biofeedback during swallowing treatment

Limitations

  • The oral phase is not directly visualized.
  • White-out obscures the moment of the pharyngeal swallow.
  • Upper esophageal opening is not directly visualized.
  • Nasal passage of the scope may be uncomfortable.
  • Patient tolerance and medical considerations must be assessed.
  • Specialized equipment and trained clinicians are required.

Modified Barium Swallow Study (MBSS)

MBSS May Be Particularly Useful For

  • Oral bolus preparation and transport
  • Timing and coordination of swallowing
  • Hyolaryngeal movement
  • Pharyngeal clearance
  • Penetration and aspiration
  • Timing of airway invasion
  • Upper esophageal opening
  • Assessment of swallowing biomechanics
  • Effects of bolus volume, consistency, and strategies
  • Visualization across lateral and anterior-posterior views

Limitations

  • Requires radiation exposure.
  • Usually requires transport to radiology.
  • Represents a limited sample of swallowing performance.
  • Secretions are not directly visualized.
  • Vocal fold movement is not directly assessed.
  • Positioning and medical stability may affect feasibility.

  1. Why might bedside findings fail to identify aspiration in some patients?
  2. How can critical illness affect swallowing function, even after respiratory status improves?
  3. What additional challenges may influence assessment decisions in the ICU?
  4. How does the clinical question influence the selection of FEES versus MBSS?
  5. When might both assessments provide complementary information?

Clinical Reasoning Reminder

An instrumental assessment is not automatically necessary for every patient.

The decision should consider whether the results are likely to provide clinically meaningful information that influences diagnosis, management, or recommendations.

Choose the Assessment

For each scenario, identify the clinical question and select the most appropriate assessment approach.

Remember that more than one approach may be reasonable depending on the patient’s presentation and the information needed.

Scenario 1: ICU and Secretion Management

A patient in the ICU has copious secretions, a weak voice, and cannot safely leave the unit.

Consider: - What information is missing? - What assessment can be completed at bedside? - What findings would influence management?

Reveal Discussion

FEES may be particularly useful.

FEES allows direct visualization of secretion management, laryngeal anatomy, airway protection, and pharyngeal residue.

A clinical evaluation remains important for determining medical stability, participation, and assessment readiness.

Scenario 2: Postoperative Oral Phase Concerns

A patient following partial glossectomy demonstrates prolonged oral preparation and suspected reduced bolus propulsion.

Consider: - Which phase of swallowing is the primary concern? - What information cannot be obtained through bedside observation? - How might surgical changes influence assessment selection?

Reveal Discussion

MBSS may be particularly useful.

MBSS allows visualization of oral bolus preparation, oral transport, and bolus flow across swallowing phases.

FEES may also provide useful information if secretion management, laryngeal function, or bedside feasibility is the primary concern.

Assessment selection should consider postoperative restrictions and the specific clinical question.

Scenario 3: Acute Stroke

A patient with an acute stroke is alert, medically stable, and able to participate. The patient has not yet completed a swallowing screen or evaluation.

Consider: - What should occur before initiating oral intake? - What is the purpose of a swallowing screen? - When is a comprehensive SLP evaluation indicated?

Reveal Discussion

Begin with the appropriate institutional stroke swallowing screening pathway.

Patients with acute stroke should undergo swallowing screening before oral intake.

A failed screen or other clinical concerns may indicate the need for a comprehensive SLP evaluation.

Instrumental assessment may be indicated based on the clinical presentation and unanswered questions.

Scenario 4: Possible Silent Aspiration

A patient demonstrates no coughing with liquids but has recurrent pneumonia and an unexplained oxygen requirement.

Consider: - Does the absence of coughing rule out aspiration? - What information would help clarify swallowing physiology? - Which instrumental assessment would be most appropriate?

Reveal Discussion

Consider instrumental swallowing assessment.

The absence of coughing does not exclude aspiration, including silent aspiration.

FEES or MBSS may be appropriate depending on the clinical question, transport feasibility, and patient status.

Recurrent pneumonia and oxygen requirements are not specific to aspiration and require broader medical consideration.

Scenario 5: Limited Transport Tolerance

A patient can tolerate only several minutes of transportation and positioning but requires repeated assessment of secretion management and airway protection.

Consider: - Which assessment is most feasible? - What information is needed? - How might repeated assessment influence management?

Reveal Discussion

FEES may be preferred.

FEES can be completed at bedside, directly visualizes secretions, and can be repeated without radiation exposure.

Medical stability, patient tolerance, and the clinical question must still guide assessment decisions.

Final Reflection

Discuss with your group:

  1. What is one clinical question that FEES can answer particularly well?
  2. What is one clinical question that MBSS can answer particularly well?
  3. Why might an apparently normal bedside assessment fail to identify swallowing impairment?
  4. How does the patient’s medical status influence assessment selection?
  5. How might instrumental findings change your treatment or diet recommendations?

Clinical Pearl

Choose the assessment that best answers the clinical question.

The goal is not simply to identify aspiration, but to understand swallowing function sufficiently to guide individualized clinical management.
